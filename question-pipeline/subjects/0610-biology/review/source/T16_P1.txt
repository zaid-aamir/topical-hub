M|16.1.1|One parent produces offspring without gametes. Apart from mutation, which feature is expected of the offspring?|The offspring have the same genetic information as the parent.|Each offspring has half the parent's genes.|Every offspring has genes from two parents.|All offspring must have different genes.|Asexual reproduction uses one parent and produces genetically identical offspring. Neither gamete fusion nor the mixing of two parents' genes occurs.
M|16.1.2|A single-celled organism divides into two cells without fusion with another cell. Which process has occurred?|asexual reproduction|pollination|fertilisation|sexual reproduction|Division from one parent without gamete fusion is asexual reproduction. Pollination transfers pollen; fertilisation and sexual reproduction involve gamete nuclei.
M|16.1.3|A grower wants to multiply one plant with a particularly desirable fruit flavour. Which advantage most directly supports using asexual reproduction?|The desirable genetic combination can be retained in the offspring.|The offspring must have increased genetic variation.|The offspring can only be formed after cross-pollination.|Every offspring becomes resistant to all diseases.|Cloning retains the parent's genetic combination. It does not create broad variation or guarantee disease resistance, and it does not require pollination.
M|16.2.1|Which event distinguishes sexual reproduction from asexual reproduction?|fusion of two gamete nuclei|increase in cell number|development of offspring|growth of a new organism|Sexual reproduction involves fusion of gamete nuclei. Growth, cell multiplication and production of offspring also occur in asexual reproduction.
M|16.2.2|A pollen grain lands on a stigma, a tube grows, and a pollen nucleus joins a nucleus in an ovule. Which event is fertilisation?|the joining of the two nuclei|the arrival of pollen on the stigma|the opening of the flower|the growth of the pollen tube alone|Fertilisation is nuclear fusion. Landing on the stigma is pollination, while tube growth enables the nuclei to meet but is not itself fertilisation.
M|16.2.3|In a species, each gamete nucleus has 12 chromosomes. How many chromosomes does the zygote nucleus have immediately after normal fertilisation?|24|6|12|48|Two haploid sets of 12 combine, giving 12 + 12 = 24 chromosomes. Fertilisation adds sets rather than halving or doubling each set separately.
M|16.3.2|Which flower structure produces pollen grains?|anther|stigma|ovary|petal|The anther produces pollen. The stigma receives it, the ovary contains ovules, and petals may attract pollinators.
M|16.3.4|Which pollen feature is most likely to help attachment to an insect's body?|a spiky outer surface|a very smooth surface that prevents attachment|a low mass that allows prolonged wind transport|a shape that prevents contact with insects|A spiky surface helps pollen attach to an insect. Light, smooth grains are more characteristic of wind dispersal; preventing contact would hinder insect pollination.
M|16.3.5|During pollination, pollen grains move between which structures?|anther and stigma|stigma and ovule|ovule and ovary|petal and sepal|Pollination transfers pollen from an anther to a stigma. Movement to an ovule occurs later, involving a pollen tube rather than transfer of whole grains.
M|16.3.7|Which combination is most typical of a wind-pollinated flower?|exposed anthers and a large feathery stigma|nectar and strongly scented petals|sticky pollen and brightly coloured petals|an enclosed stigma and very little pollen|Exposed anthers release pollen into moving air and a feathery stigma traps airborne grains. Nectar, scent and showy petals chiefly attract animals.
M|16.3.8|Viable seeds are kept at a suitable temperature with oxygen available. Which treatment prevents germination because a required condition is absent?|keeping the seeds completely dry|placing the seeds on moist cotton wool|keeping the seeds moist in darkness|supplying moist seeds with air|Water is required for germination. Light is not a universal requirement, so darkness alone does not prevent all viable seeds from germinating.
M|16.3.9|Pollen moves from one flower to another flower on the same plant. How is this classified?|self-pollination|cross-pollination|asexual reproduction|seed dispersal|Both flowers belong to the same plant, so this is self-pollination. Cross-pollination requires a different plant of the same species.
M|16.3.10|Which transfer is cross-pollination?|pollen moving between flowers on two different plants of the same species|pollen moving between two flowers on the same plant|pollen moving within one flower|seeds moving between two habitats|Cross-pollination transfers pollen to a flower on another plant of the same species. Neither within-plant transfer nor seed movement fits this definition.
M|16.3.11|A flowering plant species becomes more dependent on cross-pollination. Which potential disadvantage follows?|Successful reproduction may depend more on pollen transfer between plants.|Every offspring becomes genetically identical.|Fertilisation no longer requires a pollen nucleus.|Seeds no longer contain an embryo.|Cross-pollination requires transfer between plants, so reproduction can become vulnerable to failure of that transfer. It does not abolish fertilisation or embryo formation.
M|16.3.12|After a compatible pollen grain germinates, which route does the pollen tube take?|through the style towards an ovule|through the sepal towards an anther|from the ovule to the petal|from the filament into a leaf|The tube grows from the stigma through the style towards an ovule, carrying the male nucleus. The other routes do not deliver it to the female nucleus.
S|16.1.3|Two fields contain plants of one crop species. Field X contains clones of one parent, while field Y contains sexually produced plants.|Explain one advantage of the plants in field X for harvesting a uniform crop.|The plants have the same genetic information for relevant inherited features.~They are therefore likely to produce similar crop characteristics under the same conditions.|Explain one risk to field X if a new infectious disease arrives.|The plants have little genetic variation.~If the cloned genotype is susceptible, many plants may be affected together.
S|16.1.1,16.1.3|A rare plant grows alone in a newly formed habitat where there are no other plants of its species.|Explain why asexual reproduction can allow its population to increase quickly.|Only one parent is required, so no mate is needed.~Offspring can be produced without the delay of finding another reproductive individual.|Explain why a population produced this way may respond poorly to a major environmental change.|The offspring are genetically identical apart from mutation.~There may be no individuals with inherited features suited to the changed conditions.
S|16.2.1,16.2.2|Two gametes fuse and the resulting cell develops into an offspring.|Describe fertilisation and identify the resulting cell.|Fertilisation is fusion of the nuclei of two gametes.~The resulting cell is a zygote.|Describe two genetic features expected of offspring produced sexually.|Each offspring receives genetic information from both gametes.~Offspring are genetically different from one another.
S|16.2.3|A species has 18 chromosomes in a normal body-cell nucleus.|State the chromosome number in a gamete and explain your answer.|A gamete has 9 chromosomes.~A gamete is haploid and has one set, half the diploid body-cell number.|State the chromosome number in the zygote and explain how it is restored.|The zygote has 18 chromosomes.~The two gamete nuclei contribute 9 chromosomes each during fusion.
S|16.2.4|A crop breeder uses sexual reproduction to produce plants for a region with a changing climate.|Explain one potential advantage of this method.|Sexual reproduction produces genetic variation among offspring.~Some offspring may possess a combination of alleles better suited to the new climate.|Explain two disadvantages for producing a uniform crop rapidly.|The offspring may not retain all desirable parental characteristics.~Time and resources are needed for sexual reproduction and subsequent selection.
S|16.2.4|A wild animal population produces genetically different offspring sexually.|Explain how variation may help when a new predator arrives.|Some individuals may have inherited features that help them avoid the predator.~These individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce.|Explain why sexual reproduction can fail when suitable individuals become widely separated.|Individuals must obtain compatible gametes, normally by finding a mate.~Separation reduces the chance of mating and therefore successful fertilisation.
S|16.3.2|A flower has a damaged stigma but intact anthers and ovary.|State the functions of the stigma and the ovary.|The stigma receives pollen grains.~The ovary contains ovules.|Suggest why this damage could reduce seed production without reducing pollen production.|The damaged stigma may receive less pollen, reducing successful fertilisation.~The intact anthers can still produce pollen grains.
S|16.3.4,16.3.7|Flower A is brightly coloured and produces nectar. Flower B has small dull petals and exposed hanging anthers.|Identify the likely pollinating agent of each flower and give one reason for each identification.|A is insect-pollinated because its nectar or colour attracts insects.~B is wind-pollinated because exposed hanging anthers release pollen into air.|Describe two differences expected between their pollen grains.|A is likely to have stickier or spikier pollen than B.~B is likely to produce smaller, lighter pollen grains than A.
S|16.3.5,16.3.6|Pollen reaches a flower but no seed subsequently develops.|Distinguish pollination from fertilisation.|Pollination is transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma.~Fertilisation is fusion of a pollen nucleus with a nucleus in an ovule.|Suggest two reasons why pollen arrival might not lead to fertilisation.|The pollen may be incompatible with the receiving flower.~The pollen grain may fail to germinate or form a functioning pollen tube.
S|16.3.8|A student compares equal numbers of viable seeds from the same species. Set A is moist, aerated and at 22 °C. Set B is dry, aerated and at 22 °C.|Identify the condition being tested and state the expected difference in germination.|The condition being tested is water availability.~More seeds are expected to germinate in A than in B.|State two conditions that must be kept the same to make this comparison valid.|The temperature must be the same.~The availability of oxygen must be the same.
S|16.3.8|A student places moist seeds in two containers at the same suitable temperature. One contains air; the other contains an oxygen-free gas.|State the expected result and the conclusion if only seeds supplied with air germinate.|Seeds in air germinate while those without oxygen do not.~Oxygen is required for germination under these conditions.|Suggest two further controls needed for a fair comparison.|Use seeds of the same species and similar viability.~Provide the same water availability in both containers.
S|16.3.9,16.3.10|Pollen is transferred from flower X to flower Y on the same plant, and from flower X to flower Z on another plant of the same species.|Classify each transfer and justify the classification.|X to Y is self-pollination because both flowers are on one plant.~X to Z is cross-pollination because the flowers are on different plants of the same species.|Describe the starting and receiving structures in both transfers.|The pollen starts on an anther.~It is received by a stigma.
S|16.3.11|Two populations of the same plant species differ in their usual pollination pattern. Population P mostly self-pollinates; population Q mostly cross-pollinates.|Explain which population is more likely to produce a wider range of inherited combinations.|Q is more likely to have a wider range of combinations.~Cross-pollination combines genetic information from different plants.|Explain how the populations might differ if pollinating insects become scarce.|P may still reproduce if self-pollination can occur without insects.~Q may produce fewer seeds because pollen transfer between plants is reduced.
S|16.3.12,16.3.6|A compatible pollen grain is present on a receptive stigma.|Describe the route by which its male nucleus reaches a nucleus in an ovule.|A pollen tube grows through the style towards an ovule.~The male nucleus travels along the pollen tube into the ovule.|Describe the nuclear event that follows and state its product.|The male nucleus fuses with a nucleus in the ovule.~This forms a zygote.
S|16.3.7,16.3.8|A wind-pollinated grass produces many pollen grains.
