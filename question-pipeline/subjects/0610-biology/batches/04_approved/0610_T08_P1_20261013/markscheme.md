## Q1

Xylem transports water and mineral ions; phloem transports sucrose and amino acids. Option A puts sucrose in xylem and water in phloem, option C swaps amino acids and mineral ions, and option D reverses the two tissues completely.

## Q2
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Xylem cells are joined end to end with no cross walls, which forms a long continuous tube. Lignin (option A) makes the walls thick and strong but does not make the tube continuous. Xylem vessels have no cell contents, so they have no mitochondria (option B). Sieve plates (option C) are found in phloem, not xylem.

## Q3
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Water enters root hair cells from the soil, passes through the root cortex cells into the xylem, and the xylem carries it up the stem to the mesophyll cells of the leaf. Option B starts in the wrong cells, option C puts the cortex after the xylem, and option D uses phloem, which does not carry water up from the roots.

## Q4
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Root hairs give the root a large surface area for the uptake of water and mineral ions, so losing them reduces uptake. Option A is wrong because the roots cannot absorb more without their root hairs, whatever the volume of soil. Option B is wrong because less water enters the root, so less reaches the xylem. Option D is wrong because the cortex cells have a much smaller surface area than the root hairs that were lost.

## Q5
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Water evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces and then diffuses out through the stomata as water vapour. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour, not liquid water (options A and B), it is not active transport (option B), and the waxy cuticle reduces water loss rather than letting vapour pass out directly (option C).

## Q6
- answer: A

**Rationale**
At each temperature the bubble moves further with moving air than in still air, and at each wind condition it moves further at 25 °C than at 15 °C, so both factors increase the rate. Option B ignores the large increase with the fan, option C is the opposite of the results, and option D is wrong because the greatest distance is with moving air at 25 °C.

**Worked solution**
Compare one factor at a time. Temperature with still air: 12 mm at 15 °C and 21 mm at 25 °C, an increase. Temperature with the fan: 26 mm and 40 mm, an increase. Wind with 15 °C: 12 mm still and 26 mm with the fan, an increase. Wind with 25 °C: 21 mm and 40 mm, an increase. Both factors increase transpiration.

## Q7
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Water is pulled up the xylem by the transpiration pull, which works because forces of attraction between water molecules hold them together in a continuous column. An air gap breaks the column, so the pull cannot be passed to the water below the gap. Option A is wrong because the bubble is inside one vessel and transpiration continues. Option B is wrong because water does not move up xylem by active transport of ions. Option D is wrong because air does not remove lignin.

## Q8
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Higher humidity means more water vapour in the air outside the leaf, so the concentration gradient for diffusion of water vapour out of the stomata is smaller and transpiration slows. Options A, C and D all describe changes that increase the rate of transpiration, so none of them is a decrease.

## Q9
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in phloem from sources (parts that release them) to sinks (parts that use or store them). Option B describes water transport in xylem, option C names the wrong tissue and the wrong direction, and option D describes transpiration.

## Q10
- answer: D

**Rationale**
In late summer the leaves photosynthesise and sucrose is translocated to the tuber, which stores it as starch, so the tuber is a sink. In spring the new shoots have no leaves, so the stored starch is converted to sucrose and sent from the tuber to the shoots, making the tuber a source. Options A, B and C each get at least one of these two times wrong.

**Worked solution**
Ask at each time: does this part release sucrose (source) or use or store it (sink)? Summer: the tuber is filling up with stored food, so it is a sink. Spring: the tuber is emptying its store to feed growing shoots, so it is a source. The same organ can be a sink at one time and a source at another.

## Q16

**(a)** [4]
- water vapour is lost from the leaves by transpiration (1)
- this creates a transpiration pull (1)
- which draws water up the xylem (from the roots) (1)
- water molecules are held together by forces of attraction (between water molecules) (1)
- so water moves up as a continuous column (1)
- max 4

**(b)** [2]
- no leaves, so very little transpiration or water vapour loss (1)
- so (almost) no transpiration pull to draw water up the xylem (1)

**Worked solution**
(a) Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and leaves as water vapour through the stomata. This loss creates a transpiration pull at the top of the xylem. Because water molecules are attracted to each other, they stay together as a continuous column, so when molecules at the top are pulled, the whole column is drawn up the xylem from the roots. (b) Transpiration happens mainly from leaves. In winter the oak has no leaves, so there is very little water loss and so very little transpiration pull. Without the pull, water hardly moves up the xylem.

## Q18

**(a)** [1]
- wilting (1)

**(b)** [4]
- water is lost by transpiration faster than it is taken up by the roots (1)
- (because it is hot and dry and) the soil is dry, so little water is available to absorb (1)
- (leaf and stem) cells lose water (by osmosis) (1)
- cells become flaccid or lose turgor (pressure) (1)
- cells no longer press against each other, so the leaves and stems are no longer supported (1)
- max 4

**Worked solution**
(a) The drooping is called wilting. (b) On a hot, dry afternoon the plant loses water from its leaves by transpiration faster than its roots can take water from the dry soil. The cells of the leaves and young stems lose water by osmosis and become flaccid instead of turgid. Soft stems and leaves are held up mainly by turgid cells pressing against each other, so when the cells become flaccid the leaves and stems droop. After watering, the cells take up water, become turgid again, and the plant stands upright.

## Q19

**(a)** [1]
- a part of a plant that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids (1)

**(b)** [1]
- phloem (1)

**(c)** [4]
- in the first year the leaves photosynthesise and are the source (1)
- the root receives sucrose and stores it, so it is a sink (1)
- in the second year (early spring) the root releases the stored sucrose, so it is a source (1)
- the sucrose is translocated to the growing flowering shoot or flowers, which are sinks (1)
- because the new shoot does not yet have enough leaves to make its own food (1)
- max 4

**Worked solution**
(a) A sink is any part of a plant that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids. (b) Sucrose is translocated in the phloem. (c) A source releases sucrose and a sink uses or stores it. In the first year, sucrose made in the leaves is translocated down to the root and stored there, so the root is a sink. In the second year, the new flowering shoot grows quickly early in spring, before it has enough leaves to supply itself. The root now releases its stored sucrose, which is translocated up to the growing shoot, so the root has become a source and the shoot is a sink.

## Q20

**(a)** [2]
- sucrose or amino acids are translocated (down) from the leaves (source) in the phloem (1)
- they cannot pass the ring because the phloem is removed, so they build up above the ring (1)
- ALLOW: the build-up of food above the ring is used for growth there

**(b)** [2]
- the xylem is still intact or undamaged (1)
- so water (and mineral ions) can still be transported up to the leaves (1)

**(c)** [2]
- no sucrose or amino acids can reach the roots (because the phloem is cut) (1)
- roots need sucrose for respiration or growth, so once their stores run out they die (1)

**Worked solution**
(a) Leaves make sucrose and send it, with amino acids, down the stem in the phloem. Removing the ring of bark removes the phloem, so these substances cannot move past the ring. They build up just above it, and the extra supply makes the stem swell there. (b) The xylem was not damaged, so water and mineral ions still travel up from the roots to the leaves. The leaves keep their water supply and do not wilt. (c) The roots are sinks: they cannot photosynthesise and rely on sucrose from the leaves for respiration. With the phloem cut, no sucrose reaches them, so once their own stores are used up the roots die.
