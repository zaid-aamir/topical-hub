## Q16

**(a)** [4]
- (external) intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs upwards and outwards (1)
- diaphragm contracts and flattens / moves down (1)
- volume of the thorax increases (1)
- pressure in the thorax / lungs decreases (below atmospheric pressure), so air is pushed into the lungs (1)
- REJECT: lungs expand and suck air in (with no reference to pressure)

**(b)** [1]
- ribs move downwards and inwards (1)

**(c)** [1]
- diaphragm relaxes and moves up / becomes dome-shaped (1)

**Worked solution**
(a) To breathe in, the external intercostal muscles between the ribs contract and pull the ribcage upwards and outwards. At the same time the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards. Both movements increase the volume of the thorax. A larger volume means a lower pressure, so the pressure inside the lungs falls below the pressure of the air outside and air moves in through the trachea. (b) The internal intercostal muscles pull the ribs downwards and inwards, which reduces the volume of the thorax and forces air out quickly. (c) When breathing out, the diaphragm relaxes and returns to its dome shape, moving upwards and reducing the volume of the thorax.

## Q17

**(a)** [2]
- volume (of the thorax) increases (1)
- pressure (in the thorax) decreases (1)

**(b)** [3]
- air enters the thorax through the hole (when the chest expands) (1)
- so the pressure around the left lung does not fall / stays the same as the air outside (1)
- so there is no pressure difference to push air into the left lung (1)
- ALLOW: the left lung collapses

**Worked solution**
(a) When a healthy person breathes in, the ribs move up and out and the diaphragm flattens, so the volume of the thorax increases and the pressure inside it decreases. Air outside is now at a higher pressure, so it moves into the lungs. (b) This only works because the thorax is airtight. With a hole in the chest wall, air rushes in through the hole as the thorax gets bigger, so the pressure around the left lung stays the same as the pressure outside. With no lower pressure around it, nothing makes air move down the trachea into the left lung, so it does not fill (it collapses). The right lung is in a separate airtight space, so it can still work.

## Q18

**(a)** [2]
- oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood (1)
- oxygen is carried away by the blood / used in (aerobic) respiration by cells, so the concentration in the blood stays lower (1)

**(b)** [2]
- carbon dioxide is produced by (aerobic) respiration in cells / tissues and carried to the lungs in the blood (1)
- carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the air in the alveoli (1)

**(c)** [2]
- the air in the trachea and bronchi has the same composition as inspired air / has not taken part in gas exchange (1)
- it mixes with alveolar air, raising the oxygen percentage and lowering the carbon dioxide percentage (1)
- ALLOW: alveolar air is diluted by inspired air

**Worked solution**
(a) The blood arriving at the alveoli has a low oxygen concentration because cells have used oxygen for aerobic respiration. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood, down its concentration gradient, so the air left in the alveoli has less oxygen than the air breathed in. (b) Respiration in cells all over the body produces carbon dioxide, which the blood carries to the lungs. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the blood is higher than in the alveoli, so it diffuses into the alveoli. (c) The last air breathed in fills the trachea and bronchi but never reaches the alveoli, so it still has the composition of inspired air (21% oxygen, 0.04% carbon dioxide). When the person breathes out, this air mixes with the alveolar air on the way out, so expired air has a little more oxygen (16% not 14%) and a little less carbon dioxide (4% not 5%) than the air in the alveoli.

## Q20

**(a)** [2]
- goblet cells make / secrete mucus (1)
- mucus traps pathogens / bacteria / dust particles (1)

**(b)** [2]
- cilia beat / waft / move (1)
- moving mucus (with the trapped pathogens and particles) up to the throat (where it is swallowed) (1)
- REJECT: cilia trap / kill pathogens

**(c)** [2]
- mucus (with trapped pathogens) is not moved out of the airways / builds up in the bronchi and lungs (1)
- pathogens stay in the breathing system and can multiply / reach the alveoli, causing infection (1)

**Worked solution**
(a) Goblet cells in the lining of the trachea and bronchi make mucus. The sticky mucus traps pathogens, such as bacteria, and particles, such as dust, in the air breathed in. (b) Ciliated cells have tiny hair-like cilia on their surface. The cilia beat together and sweep the mucus, with whatever it has trapped, up the trachea to the throat, where it is swallowed. (c) If the cilia cannot move, the mucus is not carried away. It stays in the bronchi and lungs with the pathogens trapped in it. These pathogens have time to multiply and cause infections, so people with this condition get many more lung infections.
