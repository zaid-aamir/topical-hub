## Q2
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Neurones carry information as electrical impulses. Hormones are chemicals carried in the blood, not along neurones. Heat energy and oxygen molecules are not the way information is carried in the nervous system.

## Q3
- answer: D

**Rationale**
The receptor detects the stimulus, the sensory neurone carries impulses to the spinal cord, the relay neurone passes them on to the motor neurone, and the motor neurone carries impulses to the effector (an arm muscle). Option A reverses the three neurones, option B runs the whole arc backwards, and option C puts the relay neurone before the sensory neurone.

**Worked solution**
Follow the impulse from where the stimulus is detected to where the response happens: receptor (in the skin) → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector (muscle that pulls the hand away). The order of the words "sensory, relay, motor" matches "detect, pass on, act".

## Q8
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Neurotransmitter is stored in vesicles only in the neurone before the gap, and receptor proteins are only on the neurone after the gap, so transmission can only go one way. If receptors were on both neurones (option A) or both neurones released neurotransmitter (option B), impulses could cross in either direction. **Worked solution**
For an impulse to cross, neurotransmitter must be released on one side and must bind to receptors on the other side. Only one side has the vesicles and only the other side has the receptors, so the "message" can only be sent one way.

## Q13
- answer: A

**Rationale**
For a near object the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments become loose (lower tension) and the lens becomes more rounded so it refracts light more. Option B describes focusing on a distant object. Options C and D mix the two: contracted ciliary muscles cannot keep the ligaments tight, and relaxed ciliary muscles pull the ligaments tight rather than loose.

**Worked solution**
The ciliary muscles form a ring around the lens. When the ring contracts it gets smaller, so it stops pulling on the suspensory ligaments; the ligaments go slack and the elastic lens springs into a fatter, more rounded shape. A more rounded lens bends light more, which is needed for light from a near object to focus on the retina.

## Q15
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Cones are packed most densely at the centre of the retina (the fovea), and rods are more common towards the edges. ## Q22

**(a)** [4]
- impulse (arriving at the end of the sensory neurone) stimulates the release of neurotransmitter (1)
- (neurotransmitter is released) from vesicles into the synaptic gap (1)
- neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap (1)
- (neurotransmitter) binds with receptor proteins on the relay neurone (1)
- ALLOW: "an impulse is then stimulated in the relay neurone" as an alternative to any one of the first two marks if the sequence is otherwise correct

**(b)** [1]
- takes longer, because the neurotransmitter has further to diffuse (1)

**Worked solution**

## Q23

**(a)** [2]
- (drug P) has a shape that fits / is complementary to (the shape of) the receptor protein (1)
- (because) it has the same / similar shape as the neurotransmitter, which normally binds to the receptor (1)

**(b)** [2]
- neurotransmitter cannot bind to the receptor proteins / receptor proteins are blocked (1)
- so fewer / no impulses are stimulated in the next neurone / transmission is reduced or stopped (1)

**(c)** [2]
- no neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic gap / no neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap (1)
- so no neurotransmitter binds with the receptor proteins (on the next neurone) (1)
- ALLOW: impulse cannot cross the synapse, for the second mark only if linked to lack of neurotransmitter

**Worked solution**
(a) Receptor proteins only bind molecules of a matching shape. Drug P has almost the same shape as the neurotransmitter, so it fits the receptor protein just as the neurotransmitter does.
(b) While drug P is attached, the receptor proteins are occupied, so the real neurotransmitter has nowhere to bind. Drug P does not itself start an impulse, so fewer (or no) impulses are produced in the next neurone.
(c) The next neurone only produces an impulse when neurotransmitter binds to its receptor proteins. If drug Q stops release from the vesicles, no neurotransmitter crosses the gap, nothing binds to the receptors and no impulse is stimulated.

## Q24

**(a)** [2]
- impulse produced in Z (1)
- no impulse produced in X (1)

**(b)** [3]
- vesicles (of neurotransmitter) are only in the neurone before the gap / at the Y–Z synapse, Y has vesicles so it releases neurotransmitter (1)
- receptor proteins are only on the neurone after the gap / Z has receptor proteins, so neurotransmitter binds and an impulse starts in Z (1)
- at the X–Y synapse, Y has no vesicles to release neurotransmitter / X has no receptor proteins, so no impulse is produced in X (1)
- ALLOW: synapses ensure impulses travel in one direction only, for one mark if no other mark is scored

**Worked solution**
(a) The impulse reaches both ends of neurone Y, but it can only cross the synapse that leads to Z. So an impulse appears in Z but not in X.
(b) At a synapse, only the neurone before the gap stores neurotransmitter in vesicles, and only the neurone after the gap has receptor proteins. At the Y–Z synapse, Y is the neurone before the gap: it releases neurotransmitter, which diffuses across and binds to receptors on Z, so Z produces an impulse. At the X–Y synapse, Y is the neurone after the gap: it has receptor proteins but no vesicles, so it cannot release neurotransmitter towards X, and X has no receptors to respond. This is why synapses make impulses travel in one direction only.

## Q30

**(a)** [2]
- most cones / highest concentration of cones at the centre (of the retina) / at R (1)
- (very) few cones away from the centre / at P, Q and T (1)
- ALLOW: quoted data, e.g. 150 thousand per mm^2 at R but only 5–8 thousand per mm^2 at P, Q and T, for either mark

**(b)** [1]

**(c)** [2]

**(d)** [1]

**Worked solution**
(a) Cones are packed most densely at the centre of the retina (150 thousand per mm^2 at R). Away from the centre there are very few cones, only 5 to 8 thousand per mm^2 at P, Q and T.
(b) Position S has no light receptors at all. This is where the optic nerve leaves the eye, called the blind spot.

(d) There are three kinds of cone, and each absorbs light of a different colour. The brain compares the signals from the three kinds to produce colour vision.
