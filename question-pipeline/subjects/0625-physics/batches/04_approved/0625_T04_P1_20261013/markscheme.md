## Q1
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Unlike poles attract, so an S pole must face the N pole at the back of X. An N pole (A) would repel it. C is wrong because magnets do not always attract each other: like poles repel. D is wrong because magnets attract the opposite poles of other magnets as well as unmagnetised magnetic materials.

**Worked solution**
Like poles repel and unlike poles attract. The back of X is an N pole, so the front of Y needs an S pole to be attracted.

## Q2
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Only a magnet can repel a magnet, because repulsion needs like poles; so repulsion of the N pole proves the bar is magnetised. Attraction (A, B) happens with an unmagnetised magnetic material as well as with a magnet, so it proves nothing. No effect at all (C) shows a non-magnetic material.

**Worked solution**
An unmagnetised piece of iron is attracted by either pole of a magnet, and so is the opposite pole of another magnet. Attraction alone therefore cannot tell you whether the bar is a magnet. Repulsion is the only sure test: it needs like poles, and only a magnet has poles of its own.

## Q3
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Soft iron is easily magnetised and easily loses its magnetism, so it makes temporary magnets. Steel keeps its magnetism, so it makes permanent magnets; B, C and D swap or misstate these properties.

**Worked solution**
Remember the pair: soft iron = temporary (magnetises and demagnetises easily), steel = permanent (harder to magnetise but keeps its magnetism).

## Q4
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Iron, nickel and cobalt are all magnetic materials. Copper (A), aluminium (B) and brass (D) are non-magnetic, so those lists each contain a non-magnetic material.

**Worked solution**
Magnetic materials are attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Most other metals, including copper, aluminium and brass, are non-magnetic.

## Q5
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Field lines point away from an N pole, and the compass N pole points along the field, so beyond the east end it points east, away from the magnet. B wrongly reverses the field direction; C and D assume the needle points along north–south regardless of the magnet.

**Worked solution**
The direction of a magnetic field at a point is the direction of the force on an N pole there. The compass N pole is repelled by the magnet's N pole, so it is pushed further east and the needle points east.

## Q6

## Q8
- answer: D

**Rationale**
The magnet induces magnetism in the first nail: its head becomes an S pole (attracted to the N pole) and its point becomes an N pole, which in turn attracts the second nail. A is wrong because iron is a temporary magnet; B ignores the induced magnet; C confuses magnetism with electric charge.

**Worked solution**
When a magnetic material is near a magnet it becomes an induced magnet. The end nearest the magnet's N pole becomes an S pole, so the far end is an N pole. This N pole attracts the second nail, which is also induced, so a chain of nails can hang from the magnet.

## Q9

## Q10

Charging a solid by friction only moves electrons. The rod becomes negative, so it gains electrons from the cloth. A and B are wrong because positive charges do not move; C would make the rod positive.

## Q11
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Metals conduct because some electrons are free to move through the metal and carry charge. A describes an insulator; protons (B) are fixed in the nuclei; copper atoms do have electrons (D).

**Worked solution**
In a metal, some electrons are not attached to any one atom. These free electrons can drift through the metal when a voltage is applied, which is what makes copper a good conductor. In an insulator, the electrons are held to their atoms and cannot move through the material.

Each kind of field is named after what feels the force in it: charges in an electric field, magnetic poles in a magnetic field, masses in a gravitational field.

## Q13

## Q14
- answer: D

**Rationale**
The field around an isolated charged sphere is radial. Field lines show the direction of the force on a positive charge, which is attracted to a negative sphere, so the lines point towards it. B is the pattern for a positive sphere; A describes the field between parallel plates; C is not an electric field pattern for a sphere.

**Worked solution**
Outside a charged conducting sphere the field lines are straight and radial, like spokes of a wheel. Their direction is the way a positive charge would be pushed: away from a positive sphere, towards a negative one.

## Q15
- answer: C

The field of a positive point charge points radially away from it. A negative particle feels a force opposite to the field direction, so it is pulled towards the point charge (unlike charges attract). A has the force wrong; B and D have the field direction wrong.

**Worked solution**

## Q16

**(a)** [1]
- a magnetic material is attracted by a magnet / can be magnetised, but a non-magnetic material is not attracted / cannot be magnetised (1)
- ALLOW: magnetic materials are affected by magnets; non-magnetic materials are not

**(b)** [3]
- aluminium: no attraction or repulsion at either end / no effect (1)
- iron: both ends are attracted (1)
- steel magnet: one end is attracted and the other end is repelled (1)
- ALLOW: steel magnet: repelled at its N pole end and attracted at its S pole end
- REJECT: for iron, "repelled"

**Worked solution**
(a) A magnetic material (such as iron, steel, nickel or cobalt) is attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised. A non-magnetic material (such as aluminium) is not attracted and cannot be magnetised.
(b) The aluminium rod is non-magnetic, so nothing happens at either end. The unmagnetised iron rod has magnetism induced in it, so whichever end is tested is attracted. The steel magnet has its own poles: its S pole end is attracted to the N pole, and its N pole end is repelled. Testing both ends is what tells the iron and the magnet apart, because only a magnet can show repulsion.

## Q17

**(a)** [2]
- magnetism is induced in the rods / the rods become (induced) magnets because they are touching / near the magnet (1)

**(c)** [3]

## Q20

**(a)** [2]
- each magnet has its own magnetic field / the field of each magnet acts on the other magnet (1)
- the two fields interact (combine), and this interaction produces the (attractive) force on each magnet (1)
- ALLOW: field lines from the N pole of P pass into the S pole of Q, joining the two fields

**(b)** [1]
- the field is stronger between the magnets than beyond the ends / closer lines mean a stronger field (1)

**(c)** [2]
- the field lines in the gap are further apart / the field in the gap is weaker (1)
- the force between the magnets is smaller, because the interacting fields are weaker (at the greater distance) (1)
- REJECT: force becomes repulsive

**Worked solution**
(a) A magnet does not need to touch another to push or pull it. Each magnet is surrounded by a magnetic field. Where the fields of P and Q overlap they interact, and this interaction is what produces the force on each magnet. Here the field from P's N pole runs into Q's S pole, and the magnets attract.
(b) The spacing of field lines shows relative field strength: closely spaced lines mean a strong field, widely spaced lines a weak one. So the field is strongest in the gap between the magnets.
(c) With a bigger gap, each magnet's field is weaker where the other magnet is, so the lines in the gap are more widely spaced. The interaction between the fields is weaker, so the attractive force is smaller. The poles facing each other are unchanged, so the force is still attractive.

## Q21

**(a)** [1]
- N pole (1)

**(b)** [2]
- like poles (N and N) face each other, so the rings repel / there is an upward magnetic force on the upper ring (1)
- the upward magnetic force is equal to the weight of the upper ring / the forces on it are balanced (1)

**(c)** [2]
- the ring moves back up (and may bounce up and down before settling) (1)
- the repulsive force is stronger when the magnets are closer, so it is now greater than the weight (1)
- ALLOW: forces no longer balanced; upward force bigger

**(d)** [1]
- the upper ring is attracted and moves down to the lower ring / it rests on (sticks to) the lower ring (1)
- ALLOW: because unlike poles now face each other

**Worked solution**
(a) The ring floats, so it must be pushed up. Repulsion needs like poles, so the bottom face of the upper ring is an N pole, facing the N pole on top of the lower ring.
(b) The repulsion gives an upward force on the upper ring. Its weight acts downwards. When the ring is at rest these two forces are equal and opposite.
(c) Pushing the ring closer makes the magnetic force stronger, so it is bigger than the weight and the ring is pushed back up towards where the forces balance.
(d) Turning the lower ring over puts its S pole on top. Now an S pole faces an N pole, unlike poles attract, and the upper ring is pulled down onto the lower ring.

## Q30

**(a)** [1]

**(b)** [2]
- electrons are transferred (by friction) (1)
- from the hair to the comb (1)
- REJECT: positive charges move from comb to hair

**(c)** [2]
- every strand has the same (positive) charge (1)
