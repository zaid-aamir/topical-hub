## Q1
- answer: C

**Rationale**
A burette delivers a variable volume a little at a time (using its tap) and is read to the nearest 0.05 cm3. A gas syringe measures volumes of gas, a measuring cylinder is much less precise, and a volumetric pipette delivers only one fixed volume (for example 25.0 cm3).

**Worked solution**
Think about what each piece of apparatus is designed to do. A volumetric pipette measures one fixed volume very accurately, but you cannot use it to add "a little at a time" and read how much has been added. A measuring cylinder can measure different volumes, but its scale is too coarse to read to 0.05 cm3. A gas syringe measures the volume of a gas. A burette has a tap so liquid can be added drop by drop, and its scale can be read to the nearest 0.05 cm3, which is why it is used in titrations.

**Rationale**
A saturated solution contains the maximum concentration of solute that can dissolve in the solvent at a specified temperature. C is wrong because the maximum amount that dissolves depends on temperature. A and D describe amounts of solute compared with solvent, which has nothing to do with saturation.

"Saturated" means no more solute can dissolve: the solution holds the maximum concentration of solute. ## Q4
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Pencil (graphite) is insoluble in the solvent, so the baseline stays where it is. Ink contains dyes that would dissolve, move up the paper and mix with the spots being separated. A, C and D give reasons that are not true.

**Worked solution**
The baseline marks where the samples start, so that distances can be measured. Ink is itself a mixture of soluble dyes. If the line were drawn in ink, these dyes would dissolve in the solvent and travel up the paper, producing extra spots that confuse the results. Pencil does not dissolve, so the line stays put and does not interfere. The pencil has no effect on how fast the solvent rises and does not react with the substances.

## Q5
- answer: C

**Rationale**
Colourless substances cannot be seen on the paper, so after the chromatogram is dried it is sprayed with a locating agent, which makes the spots visible. Larger spots of a colourless substance are still colourless (A); an ink baseline would add unwanted dyes (B); if the substances were insoluble they would not move at all (D).

**Worked solution**
Chromatography separates the colourless substances just as it would separate coloured ones, but you cannot see where they have gone. The answer is to treat the dried chromatogram with a locating agent. This reacts with the substances to produce coloured spots, so their positions can be seen and marked. Using more sample does not make a colourless substance coloured. A solvent in which the substances are insoluble would mean nothing moves off the baseline, so there would be no separation.

## Q6
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Ethanol and water are miscible liquids with different boiling points, so they are separated by fractional distillation. Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, crystallisation obtains a solid from a solution, and chromatography separates small amounts of dissolved substances such as dyes.

**Worked solution**
Look at the information given. Both substances are liquids and they mix completely, so there is no solid to filter off or crystallise. Their boiling points are different (78 °C and 100 °C). Liquids that mix but have different boiling points are separated by fractional distillation: the liquid with the lower boiling point (ethanol) reaches the top of the fractionating column and distils over first.

**Worked solution**

K has the higher boiling point, so its vapour condenses on the cooler parts of the column and drips back into the flask. J has the lower boiling point, so its vapour reaches the top of the column, passes into the condenser, is cooled back to a liquid and is collected.

(c) While J is distilling, the thermometer at the top of the column stays at J's boiling point, 81 °C. Once all of J has distilled over, K vapour starts to reach the top of the column, so the temperature rises towards 111 °C. The rise in temperature tells the chemist that J has all been collected.

(d) A pure liquid boils at one fixed temperature. If the first liquid boils sharply at 81 °C, it is pure J. If it contains an impurity (for example some K), it will boil over a range of temperatures rather than at exactly 81 °C.
