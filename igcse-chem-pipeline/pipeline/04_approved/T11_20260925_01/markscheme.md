## Q1

C5H12 fits CnH2n+2 with n = 5 (2 × 5 + 2 = 12), so it is an alkane. C5H10 fits CnH2n (an alkene), C5H11OH fits CnH2n+1OH (an alcohol) and C4H9COOH fits CnH2n+1COOH (a carboxylic acid).

## Q2
- answer: C

**Rationale**
CH2=CHCH3 (propene) has a carbon–carbon double bond, so it is unsaturated. Propane and ethanol contain only single bonds. Ethanoic acid contains a C=O double bond, but its only carbon–carbon bond is single, so it is saturated.

**Worked solution**
"Unsaturated" means that one or more **carbon–carbon** bonds are not single bonds. Look only at the bonds between carbon atoms. In propene, CH2=CHCH3, the "=" is between two carbon atoms, so propene is unsaturated. A common mistake is to choose ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, because it contains a double bond. That double bond is between carbon and oxygen (C=O), not between two carbon atoms, so it does not make the compound unsaturated.

## Q3
- answer: A

**Rationale**
CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3 both have the molecular formula C5H12 but different arrangements (one branched, one unbranched), so they are structural isomers. B is the same compound (but-1-ene) written from opposite ends. C has different molecular formulae (C4H10 and C4H8). D are neighbouring members of the same homologous series (C2H6O and C3H8O).

**Worked solution**
Structural isomers need the **same molecular formula** but a **different structural formula**. Count atoms in each pair first. In A, both compounds contain 5 C and 12 H, and one has a branch (a CH3 group on the second carbon) while the other is a straight chain, so they are isomers. In B, CH3CH2CH=CH2 read backwards is CH2=CHCH2CH3: it is the same molecule, with the double bond at the end of the chain in both. In C the hydrogen counts differ (10 and 8). In D the compounds differ by a CH2 unit, which makes them homologues, not isomers.

## Q8
- answer: D

**Rationale**
The part CH3CH2COO– comes from propanoic acid (three carbon atoms, including the C of COO), so it is "propanoate". The part –CH2CH2CH2CH3 comes from butan-1-ol, so it is "butyl". The ester is butyl propanoate. A reverses the two parts; B and C miscount the carbon atoms.

**Worked solution**
An ester is named in two parts: first the part from the alcohol (ending -yl), then the part from the acid (ending -oate). In a structural formula written as R–COO–R', the acid part is R–COO (the C=O carbon belongs to the acid) and the alcohol part is the group attached to the single-bonded oxygen. Here the acid part is CH3CH2COO: three carbons, so propanoate. The alcohol part is CH2CH2CH2CH3: four carbons, so butyl. The name is butyl propanoate. A common error is to name the parts in the order they are written, which gives the wrong answer "propyl butanoate".

## Q11
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Fractions near the top of the column have shorter chains, lower boiling points (they are more volatile) and lower viscosity. B wrongly gives a higher boiling point, C reverses chain length and viscosity, and D wrongly gives a higher viscosity.

**Worked solution**
The column is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top. Only substances with low boiling points stay as vapour all the way to the top, and low boiling points go with short chains (small molecules). Short-chain fractions also flow easily, so their viscosity is low. All three properties therefore change together: going up the column, chain length decreases, boiling point decreases and viscosity decreases.

## Q14
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Replacing a hydrogen atom by a chlorine atom is substitution. Ultraviolet light provides the activation energy (it is a photochemical reaction), and the products are chloroethane and hydrogen chloride. A is wrong because this is not addition and HCl also forms. ## Q16
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Hexene is unsaturated and adds bromine, so the orange colour disappears. Hexane is saturated and does not react, so the aqueous bromine stays orange. A reverses the results, C would mean both are unsaturated, and D describes a colour appearing, which does not happen.

**Worked solution**
Aqueous bromine is orange (it is sometimes described as yellow or brown). An alkene has a C=C double bond, and bromine adds across it, forming a colourless product, so the mixture becomes colourless. An alkane has only single bonds and cannot undergo this addition reaction, so no change is seen and the mixture stays orange. This is the test that distinguishes saturated from unsaturated hydrocarbons.

**Worked solution**
In addition polymerisation, the C=C double bond of each monomer opens up and the monomers join into a long chain. Working backwards, the repeat unit –[CH2–CHCl]– has two carbon atoms in the main chain: one with two H atoms, and one with one H and one Cl. The monomer has exactly the same atoms on each carbon, joined by a double bond: CH2=CHCl (chloroethene). Always check that the groups on each carbon in the monomer match the groups in the repeat unit.

## Q27
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Condensation polymerisation forms a small molecule (such as water) at each linkage as well as the polymer; in addition polymerisation the polymer is the only product. B reverses this. C is wrong because it is addition polymerisation that needs C=C double bonds. D is wrong because amide and ester linkages are found in condensation polymers.

**Worked solution**
In addition polymerisation, monomers with a C=C double bond join together and nothing else is made, so the polymer is the only product. In condensation polymerisation, two different functional groups react (for example –COOH with –OH or with –NH2) and each time they join, a small molecule such as water is removed. The linkages formed are ester linkages (in polyesters) or amide linkages (in polyamides).
