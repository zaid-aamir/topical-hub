## Q1
- answer: C

**Rationale**
The modern Periodic Table is arranged in order of increasing proton number (atomic number), so C is correct. Mass number and number of neutrons are not used, because isotopes of the same element have different values of both. Relative atomic mass (D) is a common misconception: it usually increases along with proton number, but not always (argon, Ar = 40, comes before potassium, Ar = 39).

**Worked solution**
Every element has its own proton number, and the Periodic Table places the elements one after another as the proton number goes up by one each time: hydrogen 1, helium 2, lithium 3, and so on. Rows are called periods and columns are called groups. It is tempting to think the order is by mass, because relative atomic mass mostly rises across the table too, but there are exceptions such as argon and potassium, which are placed by proton number (18 then 19) even though argon has the larger relative atomic mass.

## Q2
- answer: D

**Rationale**
Atoms of Group VI elements have six outer electrons and gain two electrons to complete the outer shell, forming ions with a 2− charge (D). A would be a Group II ion. B and C wrongly use the group number itself as the charge; losing or gaining six electrons does not happen.

**Worked solution**
For metals in Groups I, II and III, the charge on the ion is positive and equal to the group number (1+, 2+, 3+). For non-metals in Groups V, VI and VII, the atoms gain electrons to reach a full outer shell, so the charge is negative and equal to (8 − group number): Group V gives 3−, Group VI gives 2−, Group VII gives 1−. Element X is in Group VI, so it forms X2− ions, for example oxide O2− and sulfide S2−.

## Q5
- answer: D

**Rationale**
A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. Chlorine is more reactive than iodine, so chlorine displaces iodine from potassium iodide (D). In A, B and C the halogen added is less reactive than the halogen in the halide, so there is no reaction.

**Worked solution**
Reactivity decreases down Group VII: chlorine > bromine > iodine. A halogen can only take the place of a halogen below it in the group. In D, chlorine is above iodine, so the reaction is chlorine + potassium iodide → potassium chloride + iodine. In A, iodine is below bromine; in B, bromine is below chlorine; in C, iodine is below chlorine. So none of A, B or C reacts.

## Q6
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Transition elements typically form coloured compounds, whereas Group I compounds are usually white, so B is correct. A, C and D are true of all metals, including the Group I metals, so they do not distinguish transition elements.

**Worked solution**
All metals conduct electricity, form positive ions and are shiny when freshly cut, so these properties cannot be used to tell a transition element from a Group I metal. The special properties of transition elements are: high density, high melting point, coloured compounds, and acting as catalysts (as the element or in compounds). Group I metals have low densities and low melting points, and their compounds, such as sodium chloride, are white.

## Q7

**Rationale**
Noble gas atoms have a full outer shell of electrons, so they have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, and they are unreactive (A). B is not a reason: many reactive substances, such as chlorine, are gases. C is wrong because noble gases are monatomic. D is wrong because all atoms have equal numbers of protons and electrons.

## Q22
- answer: D

**Rationale**
In FeCl2 iron is present as Fe2+ (oxidation number +2) and in FeCl3 it is Fe3+ (+3), so this pair shows iron with two different oxidation numbers (D). In A both compounds contain iron(II); in B both contain iron(III); in C both contain iron(II).

**Worked solution**
Work out the charge on the iron ion from the known charges of the other ions: Cl− is 1−, SO4 2− is 2−, O2− is 2− and S2− is 2−.
- FeCl2: 2 × (1−) = 2−, so iron is Fe2+. FeCl3: 3 × (1−) = 3−, so iron is Fe3+. Different, so D is correct.

Only D contains iron in two different oxidation states, which is what "variable oxidation numbers" means.

(a) Chlorine displaced both bromine and iodine, so it is the most reactive. Bromine displaced iodine but not chlorine, so it is in the middle. Iodine displaced neither, so it is the least reactive. Order: chlorine > bromine > iodine.

(b) A halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from its halide. Bromine is more reactive than iodine, so it pushes iodine out of potassium iodide. Bromine is less reactive than chlorine, so it cannot push chlorine out of potassium chloride and there is no change.

(c) Bromine is Br2 and iodine is I2 (halogens are diatomic). Potassium iodide is KI and potassium bromide is KBr. One Br2 needs two KI: Br2 + 2KI → 2KBr + I2. Check: 2 Br, 2 K and 2 I on each side.

(d) The iodine released makes the solution brown. (The potassium bromide formed is colourless in solution.) Note that iodide ions are colourless; it is the element iodine that is coloured.

## Q30

**(a)(i)** [1]
- FeO (1)

**(a)(ii)** [1]
- Fe2O3 (1)

**(b)** [1]
- Fe2+ (1)
- ALLOW Fe+2
- REJECT Fe3+ / Fe2−

**(c)** [2]
- CuCl: copper(I) chloride (1)
- CuCl2: copper(II) chloride (1)
- REJECT copper chloride without an oxidation number

**(d)** [1]
- iron forms ions with more than one charge / different oxidation numbers (Fe2+ and Fe3+) (1)
- ALLOW iron has variable oxidation numbers

**Worked solution**
(a) The Roman numeral in the name gives the oxidation number, which equals the charge on the iron ion. Oxide ions are O2−. Iron(II) is Fe2+: one Fe2+ balances one O2−, giving FeO. Iron(III) is Fe3+: two Fe3+ (total 6+) balance three O2− (total 6−), giving Fe2O3.

(b) In iron(II) chloride the iron has oxidation number +2, so the ion is Fe2+. (The compound is FeCl2.)

(c) Chloride ions are Cl−. In CuCl, one Cl− balances one copper ion, so copper is Cu+ with oxidation number +1: copper(I) chloride. In CuCl2, two Cl− ions balance one copper ion, so copper is Cu2+ with oxidation number +2: copper(II) chloride.

(d) Sodium, a Group I metal, only ever forms Na+ ions. Iron, a transition element, can form ions with different charges, Fe2+ and Fe3+, so it has variable oxidation numbers. This is a typical property of transition elements.
