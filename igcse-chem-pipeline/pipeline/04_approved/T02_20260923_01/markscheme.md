## Q6

**(a)** [3]
- protons and neutrons are found in the nucleus, at the centre of the atom (1)
- electrons are arranged in shells (energy levels) around the nucleus (1)
- an atom has no overall charge because the number of (positively charged) protons equals the number of (negatively charged) electrons, so the charges cancel out (1)

**(b)** [2]
- all three relative charges correct: proton +1, neutron 0, electron -1 (1)
- all three relative masses correct: proton 1, neutron 1, electron very small / about 1/1840 (1)

**Worked solution**
An atom is built from three particles. The protons and neutrons are packed together in a tiny central nucleus, while the electrons occupy shells around the outside. Protons carry a charge of +1 and electrons carry a charge of -1; because a neutral atom always has equal numbers of protons and electrons, the total positive charge exactly cancels the total negative charge, leaving no overall charge. For the table: protons and neutrons both have a relative mass of 1 (and neutrons have no charge, matching their name), while electrons have almost no mass (about 1/1840 of a proton's mass) and carry a charge of -1.

## Q9

Chemical properties depend on electron arrangement, especially the outer shell. Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons and therefore the same number of electrons, giving them identical electronic configurations and identical chemical behaviour, so C is correct. A is wrong (isotopes have different numbers of neutrons, which is exactly what makes them isotopes). B is a true fact about this pair but does not explain why their chemistry is the same. Since their electron arrangements are identical, their chemical behaviour is identical too - only their masses (and physical properties linked to mass) differ.

## Q11

**(a)** [3]
- method: multiply each isotope's mass by its percentage abundance and add the results, then divide by 100 (1)
- correct substitution: (35 x 75) + (37 x 25), all over 100 (1)

**Worked solution**
Relative atomic mass is a weighted average of the isotopic masses, weighted by how abundant each isotope is. Multiply each isotope's mass by its percentage abundance, add the results together, then divide by 100 (because the abundances are percentages): (35 x 75 + 37 x 25) / 100 = (2625 + 925) / 100 = 3550 / 100 = 35.5. ## Q13

## Q14
- answer: A

**Rationale**
Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong electrostatic forces in the giant lattice, and they conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are then free to move, so A is correct. B and D wrongly state a low melting point. C wrongly states that conductivity only happens when solid - the opposite is true, since ions cannot move in a solid lattice.

## Q15
- answer: B

**Rationale**
B correctly describes the giant ionic lattice structure of sodium chloride: a regular, repeating pattern of alternating positive and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic forces acting in all directions. A wrongly describes it as separate molecules. C wrongly suggests a small random cluster rather than a large regular lattice. D describes a giant covalent structure (like diamond), not an ionic one.

**Worked solution**
Ionic compounds do not exist as individual molecules; instead, huge numbers of positive and negative ions pack together in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern (a giant lattice), with each ion attracted electrostatically to the oppositely charged ions around it in every direction. This is what makes ionic lattices so difficult to break apart, giving ionic compounds their high melting points.

## Q16

**(a)** [2]
- the potassium atom loses one electron to form a K+ ion (1)
- the fluorine atom gains one electron to form an F- ion (1)

**(b)** [2]
- K+: 2,8,8 (1)
- F-: 2,8 (1)

**(c)** [1]
- the K+ and F- ions have opposite charges and are held together by a strong electrostatic force of attraction (1)

**Worked solution**
Potassium (2,8,8,1) has just one electron in its outer shell, so it is easiest for it to lose that one electron and reach the stable configuration 2,8,8 (the same as argon), becoming a K+ ion. Fluorine (2,7) has seven outer electrons, so it is easiest for it to gain one more electron to complete its outer shell, reaching 2,8 (the same as neon), becoming an F- ion. The electron lost by potassium is the same electron gained by fluorine. The resulting K+ and F- ions carry opposite charges, so they attract each other strongly - this electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions is the ionic bond.

## Q19

**(a)** [2]
- calcium oxide consists of a regular, repeating (three-dimensional) arrangement of alternating Ca2+ and O2− ions (1)
- the ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction acting in all directions throughout the lattice (1)

**(b)** [2]
- there are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged Ca2+ and O2− ions throughout the giant lattice (1)

**(c)** [2]

**Worked solution**

## Q22

**(a)** [2]

**(b)** [2]
- the carbon atom shares one electron with each of the four hydrogen atoms, giving the carbon atom 8 electrons in its outer shell (1)
- each hydrogen atom shares one electron with the carbon atom, giving each hydrogen atom a full outer shell of 2 electrons (1)

**Worked solution**

## Q23

**(a)** [3]
- the carbon atom shares a total of 4 electrons, 2 with each oxygen atom (1)
- each oxygen atom shares 2 electrons with the carbon atom, so 2 shared pairs of electrons (a double bond) form between the carbon atom and each oxygen atom (1)
- as a result, both the carbon atom and each oxygen atom end up with a full outer shell of 8 electrons (1)

**(b)** [2]
- 6 electrons shared / 3 shared pairs of electrons (1)
- triple (covalent) bond (1)

**Worked solution**
Carbon has 4 outer electrons and needs 4 more; each oxygen atom has 6 outer electrons and needs 2 more. In CO2, the carbon atom forms a double bond with each oxygen atom: it shares 2 of its electrons with each oxygen atom (using all 4 of its outer electrons in total), and each oxygen atom shares 2 of its own electrons back with carbon. Each double bond is therefore 2 shared pairs (4 electrons). To count outer electrons, each atom counts every electron in every shared pair around it. Carbon has 2 shared pairs with each oxygen atom, so 4 shared pairs (8 electrons) in total - a full outer shell. Each oxygen atom has 2 shared pairs with carbon (4 electrons) plus its own 4 unshared electrons, giving 8 - also a full outer shell. So all three atoms achieve a noble gas electronic configuration. In N2, each nitrogen atom has 5 outer electrons and needs 3 more, so the two atoms share 3 pairs of electrons (6 electrons in total) between them - a triple bond - giving each nitrogen atom a full outer shell of 8.

## Q24

**(a)** [3]
- iodine molecules are held together by weak intermolecular forces (forces between molecules) (1)
- the covalent bonds within each iodine molecule are strong (1)
- on melting or boiling, only the weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome, and this requires only a small amount of energy, so melting and boiling points are low (1)

**(b)** [2]

**(c)** [1]

- ALLOW: any correct statement that the intramolecular covalent bond is much stronger than the intermolecular forces

**Worked solution**
It is important not to confuse the strong covalent bonds holding the two atoms together within an iodine molecule with the much weaker forces of attraction between separate iodine molecules. ## Q27

**(a)** [2]

**(b)** [2]
- graphite consists of carbon atoms arranged in layers (of hexagonal rings), each carbon atom bonded to 3 others, with weak forces between the layers (1)
- because the forces between the layers are weak, the layers can slide over each other easily, allowing graphite to be used as a lubricant (1)

**(c)** [2]

**Worked solution**
Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon but have very different structures. ## Q28

**(a)** [2]
- each silicon atom is covalently bonded to 4 oxygen atoms, and each oxygen atom is covalently bonded to 2 silicon atoms (1)
- forming a giant covalent (three-dimensional) lattice/structure (1)

**(b)** [3]
- silicon(IV) oxide has many strong covalent bonds throughout its giant lattice, which must all be broken to melt it (1)

## Q29
- answer: C

**Rationale**
C correctly describes metallic structure: a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a "sea" of delocalised electrons that are free to move throughout the structure. A describes an ionic lattice. B describes a simple molecular structure. D describes covalently bonded atoms, not a metal.

## Q30

**(a)** [2]
- positive metal ions are arranged in a regular giant lattice (1)
- the ions are surrounded by a "sea" of delocalised electrons, which are free to move throughout the structure (1)

**(b)** [2]
- the delocalised electrons are free to move throughout the metal lattice (1)
- these mobile electrons can carry electrical charge through the structure, allowing an electric current to flow (1)

**(c)** [2]
- the layers of positive metal ions can slide over each other when a force is applied (1)
- the delocalised electrons are not fixed in position and move with the ions, continuing to hold the structure together as the ions slide, so the metallic bond is not broken and the metal bends/changes shape rather than shattering (1)

**Worked solution**
A metal is a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons - electrons that have left their original atoms and are free to move anywhere throughout the structure. This mobility explains both key properties. Electrical conductivity: because the delocalised electrons can move freely, they can flow through the metal and carry an electric current when a voltage is applied. Malleability and ductility: when a force is applied, the layers of positive ions can slide past one another into new positions; because the delocalised electrons are not tied to any particular ion, they simply move along too and continue to hold the ions together in their new arrangement. This is why metals can be hammered into sheets or drawn into wires without breaking, unlike ionic compounds, where sliding layers brings ions of the same charge together, causing them to repel and the structure to shatter.
