## Q5
- answer: C

**Rationale**
A catalyst works by opening up a different reaction pathway that has a lower activation energy, so more collisions have enough energy to be successful. It does not change the number of particles per unit volume (that would be concentration), the average kinetic energy of the particles (that would be temperature), or the pressure of the system.

**Worked solution**
Only collisions with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy lead to a reaction. By lowering the activation energy required, a catalyst means a larger proportion of the existing collisions now have enough energy to succeed, without needing to change the particles' concentration, energy or the system's pressure at all.

## Q7

**(a)** [3]
- increasing concentration increases the number of acid particles per unit volume (1)
- so collisions between acid and magnesium particles happen more frequently (1)
- more frequent collisions per second means more successful collisions (with energy ≥ activation energy) per second, so the rate increases (1)
- ALLOW: reference to "more particles in the same volume" for the first point

**(b)** [3]
- increasing temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles, so they move faster (1)
- particles collide more frequently AND with more energy per collision (1)
- a greater proportion of collisions now have energy ≥ activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful, increasing the rate (1)
- ALLOW: "more particles have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy"

**Worked solution**
(a) Collision theory says that reactions only happen when particles collide with enough energy. Increasing the concentration of the acid packs more acid particles into the same volume of solution. With more particles present, the magnesium surface is struck by acid particles more often each second, so more collisions — and therefore more successful collisions — occur per second, increasing the rate.
(b) Raising the temperature gives all the particles more kinetic energy on average, so they move around faster. This has two effects: particles collide more often (because they are moving faster), and, more importantly, a much larger fraction of those collisions now have at least the activation energy needed to react. Both effects increase the rate, but the increase in successful collisions from higher energy is the dominant one.

## Q9
- answer: B

**Rationale**
⇌ is the symbol specifically used to show that a reaction can proceed in both the forward and reverse directions. → shows a one-way (irreversible) reaction, = is used for numerical equality, and ± is not a chemical equation symbol.

**Worked solution**
Reversible reactions, where products can react to re-form the original reactants under suitable conditions, are shown using the double-headed arrow ⇌, as in N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g). A single arrow → is reserved for a reaction that only goes to completion in one direction.

## Q14

**(a)** [2]
- the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction (1)
- the concentrations of reactants and products are no longer changing (1)

**(b)** [2]
- position of equilibrium shifts towards the products / more NH3 is formed (1)
- because there are fewer gas molecules on the product side (2 mol) than the reactant side (4 mol), so increasing pressure favours the side with fewer molecules (1)
- ALLOW: "shifts to reduce the pressure/number of gas molecules"

**(c)** [2]
- position of equilibrium shifts towards the reactants / less NH3 is formed (1)
- because the forward reaction is exothermic, so increasing temperature favours the endothermic (reverse) reaction, which absorbs the extra heat (1)
- ALLOW: "shifts in the endothermic direction to oppose the change"

**(d)** [1]
- no effect on the position of equilibrium (1)
- REJECT: any answer suggesting the catalyst shifts the equilibrium; ALLOW a note that it reaches equilibrium faster, but the position (yield) is unchanged

**Worked solution**
(a) At equilibrium in a closed system, the forward and reverse reactions are still both happening, but at equal rates, so there is no further overall change in the amounts of reactants and products present — their concentrations stay constant.
(b) There are 4 moles of gas on the left (1 N2 + 3 H2) but only 2 moles of gas on the right (2 NH3). Increasing the pressure favours whichever side has fewer gas molecules, since that reduces the total pressure again, so the equilibrium shifts towards the ammonia (product) side, increasing the yield of NH3.
(c) The forward (ammonia-forming) reaction releases heat (exothermic). By Le Chatelier's principle, raising the temperature favours the reaction that absorbs heat — the reverse, endothermic reaction — so the equilibrium shifts back towards N2 and H2, reducing the yield of ammonia. (This is why industrially a compromise, moderate temperature is chosen rather than a very high one.)
(d) A catalyst speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster, but the position of equilibrium — and therefore the equilibrium yield of ammonia — is completely unaffected.
