## Q3
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Lead is a metal, so it forms at the cathode (negative electrode); bromine is a non-metal, so it forms at the anode (positive electrode) — this follows the rule that metals form at the cathode and non-metals at the anode. A swaps the electrodes round. C wrongly assumes water is present, but molten lead(II) bromide contains no water, so hydrogen and oxygen cannot form. D mixes up bromine with oxygen.

**Worked solution**
Molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr2, contains only Pb2+ and Br− ions — there is no water present. During electrolysis, Pb2+ ions move to the cathode and gain electrons to form lead metal (a grey molten bead), while Br− ions move to the anode and lose electrons to form bromine (a choking, orange-brown vapour/liquid). Since lead is a metal it must appear at the cathode, and bromine, a non-metal, at the anode, which matches option B exactly.

## Q4
- answer: B

**Rationale**
This is the general syllabus rule for electrolysis: metals (or hydrogen) always form at the cathode, and non-metals other than hydrogen always form at the anode. A is wrong because it is the metal/hydrogen, not a non-metal, that forms at the cathode. C is wrong because two metals cannot form at once from a single ionic compound — one element is always a metal (or hydrogen) and the other a non-metal. D has the electrodes swapped compared with the correct rule.

**Worked solution**
Cations (positive ions) are attracted to the cathode; in molten ionic compounds these are always metal ions (or, in some aqueous cases, hydrogen ions), so a metal or hydrogen always forms there. Anions (negative ions) are attracted to the anode; these are always non-metal ions (other than hydrogen), so a non-metal always forms there. This pattern holds for every molten ionic compound, which is exactly what option B states.

## Q5
- answer: B

**Rationale**
Dilute sulfuric acid contains H+ and SO4 2− ions (plus water). H+ ions are reduced at the cathode to give hydrogen gas; at the anode, hydroxide ions/water are oxidised to give oxygen gas (sulfate ions are not discharged). A has the gases swapped. C and D wrongly suggest sulfur-containing products, but sulfate ions remain in solution and are not converted into elemental sulfur or sulfur dioxide under these conditions.

**Worked solution**
Dilute sulfuric acid is really a dilute aqueous solution, so the electrolysis is effectively the electrolysis of water: hydrogen collects at the cathode (from reduction of H+ ions) and oxygen collects at the anode (from oxidation of water/hydroxide ions), in a 2:1 ratio by volume. The sulfate ions stay in solution throughout and become more concentrated as water is used up, but they are not themselves converted into a product, so B is the correct pairing.

## Q12

**(a)** [1]
- copper (deposited on the cathode) (1)

**(b)** [1]
- oxygen (1)

**(c)** [2]
- the copper anode dissolves (1)
- copper atoms lose electrons and enter the solution as Cu2+ ions (1)

**(d)** [3]
- with copper electrodes: Cu2+ ions are removed from solution at the cathode (as copper is deposited) (1)

In both experiments, Cu2+ ions are reduced at the cathode and copper metal is deposited there, so the cathode product is the same either way. The difference is at the anode. This is the basis of the industrial purification of copper.

## Q13
- answer: B

**Rationale**
In the external circuit, electrons are pushed by the battery away from the anode (where oxidation releases electrons into the wire) and towards the cathode (where reduction requires electrons), so overall electrons flow from anode to cathode through the wires. A and D reverse or confuse this direction. C is wrong because electrons genuinely do flow through the wires — only in the electrolyte itself is current carried by ions, not electrons.

**Worked solution**
At the anode, negative ions lose electrons (oxidation); these electrons flow away from the anode, through the external wires and the battery, and back to the cathode, where positive ions gain them (reduction). So although current directions are sometimes described differently, the electrons themselves move from the anode, through the external circuit, to the cathode — this is option B. Inside the electrolyte, by contrast, charge is carried by the ions moving towards their respective electrodes, not by free electrons.

## Q30

**(a)** [2]
- only water (vapour) leaves the exhaust (1)
- no carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide or other polluting gases are released while driving, because no carbon-based fuel is burned (there is no combustion) (1)

**(b)** [4]
- the claim is only true for emissions "at the tailpipe" while driving, not for the whole life cycle of the fuel (1)
- hydrogen does not occur freely in useful quantities and must be manufactured, e.g. by the electrolysis of water, or from natural gas/hydrocarbons (1)
- if the electricity or process used to manufacture the hydrogen comes from burning fossil fuels, carbon dioxide and other pollutants are released during production (1)
- so the "zero emissions" claim is misleading for the fuel's whole life cycle unless the hydrogen is made using a low-carbon/renewable energy source, even though the car itself is genuinely pollution-free while driving (1)
- ALLOW: valid alternative reasoning, e.g. energy/emissions involved in manufacturing or disposing of the fuel cell and storage tank

**Worked solution**
While the car is being driven, the claim is genuinely true: hydrogen and oxygen combine in the fuel cell to form only water, so the exhaust really does release no carbon dioxide or other pollutants — the car itself is pollution-free in use. However, "100% pollution-free" is a much bigger claim than that, because it ignores where the hydrogen fuel comes from. Hydrogen is not a naturally occurring fuel source; it has to be manufactured, commonly by electrolysing water or by processing natural gas, both of which require energy. If that energy comes from burning fossil fuels, then pollutant gases, including carbon dioxide, are released during hydrogen production, even though none come from the car itself. So the advertisement's claim is fair only for the car's own emissions while driving, not for the environmental impact of the whole process of making its fuel — a more honest claim would depend on how "green" the hydrogen supply actually is.
