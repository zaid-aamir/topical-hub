**(a)** [2]
- melting point = 0 °C (1)
- boiling point = 100 °C (1)
- ALLOW values read correctly from the table even if expressed as "0" and "100" without units

**(b)** [2]
- between 4 and 8 minutes: melting (solid → liquid) (1)
- between 18 and 22 minutes: boiling (liquid → gas) (1)
- REJECT "evaporating" for 18–22 minutes unless boiling is also given, since the whole liquid is changing state at a fixed temperature here

**(c)** [2]
- the energy supplied is used to weaken/overcome the forces of attraction between the particles (increasing their potential energy), allowing the fixed structure to break down (1)
- it is not used to increase the particles' kinetic energy at this point, so the temperature (which depends on kinetic energy) does not rise until melting is complete (1)
- ALLOW reference to "latent heat" being absorbed for the change of state

**Worked solution**
Reading the table, the temperature is constant at 0 °C from 4 to 8 minutes — this is the melting point, so the solid is melting into a liquid during this time. The temperature is constant again at 100 °C from 18 to 22 minutes — this is the boiling point, so the liquid is boiling into a gas here. Between these plateaus (8–18 minutes) the liquid water is simply warming up, and before 4 minutes the solid ice is warming up.

During a change of state, all the energy being supplied is used to pull the particles further apart against the forces of attraction holding them together (for example, breaking down the rigid structure of the solid). None of this energy goes into speeding the particles up further, so their average kinetic energy — and therefore the temperature — stays the same until every particle has changed state. Once melting (or boiling) is complete, further heating goes back into increasing kinetic energy, and the temperature starts rising again.

## Q13

**(a)** [1]

**(b)** [4]
- particles are close together (touching), but not held in a fixed, regular pattern like a solid (1)
- particles are arranged randomly (1)
- particles can move/slide past each other (are not fixed in position) (1)

**Worked solution**
No fixed shape but a fixed volume are the defining properties of a liquid, so substance R is a liquid. In a liquid, the particles are close together, almost as close as in a solid, which is why liquids (like solids) have a fixed volume and cannot easily be compressed. This freedom to move lets a liquid flow and change shape to fill the bottom of whatever container it is placed in, while staying the same overall volume.

## Q15

**(a)** [3]
- the gas particles gain kinetic energy as they are heated (1)
- they move faster / their average speed increases (1)
- they collide with each other and with the walls of the can more often (1)
- ALLOW "particles move more vigorously" for the second mark
- IGNORE "particles vibrate more" (gas particles do not vibrate about fixed positions)

**(b)** [2]
- the faster-moving particles hit the inside walls of the can more often and with greater force each time (1)
- the volume of the can is fixed, so this greater total force on each unit area of wall means a higher pressure (1)

**(c)** [2]
- if the pressure inside becomes greater than the can is designed to withstand (1)
- the can will burst/rupture suddenly, releasing its contents (1)
- ALLOW "the walls of the can cannot withstand the force/pressure and split open"

**Worked solution**
Heating the gas inside the sealed can increases the average kinetic energy of its particles, so they move around faster. Because the can is sealed and rigid, the volume available to the gas cannot change, so these faster particles hit the inside walls of the can more frequently and with greater force each time they collide. Aerosol cans are designed to withstand normal pressures, but if they are heated too much the pressure can exceed what the metal walls can safely contain, causing the can to burst suddenly and forcefully — which is why the warning label tells people not to heat it.

## Q17

**(a)** [3]
- a very large number of air particles are squeezed into a small, fixed volume, so the particles are close together (1)
- this means the particles collide with the cylinder walls very often (1)
- and with a large total force, giving a high pressure (1)

**(b)** [2]
- the volume occupied by this fixed mass of air increases (1)
- because at the much lower pressure in the lungs, the same particles need to spread out over a larger space to produce that lower pressure (1)

**(c)** [2]
- as a diver rises, the surrounding (and lung) pressure decreases, so any air remaining in the lungs will expand to occupy a larger volume (1)
- if the diver holds their breath, this expanding air cannot escape and can over-expand and damage the lungs; breathing out steadily lets the expanding gas escape safely (1)

**Worked solution**
Compressing a large mass of air into the small, rigid volume of a cylinder forces the gas particles much closer together than they would be at normal atmospheric pressure. With so many particles in such a small space, they collide with the cylinder walls extremely frequently and with a large total force, which is measured as a very high pressure. When some of this air is breathed in and reaches the diver's lungs, it is now at ordinary atmospheric (or slightly higher, underwater) pressure, which is much lower than the pressure inside the cylinder — so the same mass of air particles spreads out and occupies a much larger volume. As a diver swims towards the surface, the pressure around them keeps falling, so any air in their lungs keeps expanding. If they held their breath, this expanding air would have nowhere to go and could stretch and damage the lungs; breathing out continuously allows the expanding air to escape safely instead.

## Q18

**(a)** [1]

- ALLOW "both involve a liquid turning into a gas" or equivalent

**(b)** [3]
- evaporation can happen at any temperature (below the boiling point) (1)
- boiling happens only at one fixed temperature, the boiling point (1)
- evaporation happens only at the surface of the liquid whereas boiling happens throughout the whole liquid (1)
- ALLOW "bubbles of gas/vapour form inside the liquid during boiling" for the third mark
- IGNORE references to speed/rate as a "difference" unless linked to temperature or location

**Worked solution**
Both processes turn a liquid into a gas, so that is their similarity. The differences are about where and at what temperature they happen. Evaporation only happens at the exposed surface of a liquid, and it can happen at any temperature — this is why damp washing dries even on a cool, windy day, well below 100 °C. Boiling, on the other hand, happens throughout the whole liquid (bubbles of vapour form inside the liquid, not just at the surface), but only once the liquid reaches its fixed boiling point — 100 °C for pure water at normal atmospheric pressure, which is why the kettle must heat the water all the way to that temperature before it boils.

## Q20

**(b)** [3]
- the particles lose kinetic energy and slow down (1)
- the forces of attraction between the particles hold them in a fixed, regular (ordered) arrangement / lattice (1)
- the particles are no longer free to move past one another and only vibrate about fixed positions (1)
- IGNORE statements about the particles moving closer together (water expands on freezing, so do not credit or penalise)

**(c)** [2]
- the energy being removed is taken from the particles' potential energy as they form the fixed, ordered structure (bonds/attractions between particles becoming fixed), not from their kinetic energy (1)
- because temperature depends on the particles' kinetic energy, the temperature does not fall until freezing is complete and there is no more potential energy of this kind left to remove (1)
- ALLOW reference to "latent heat" being released during freezing

**Worked solution**
The water turning from a liquid into a solid is freezing. While this change is taking place, the energy removed by the freezer is being taken from the energy associated with the particles locking into this ordered structure, not from their kinetic energy. Since temperature is a measure of the particles' average kinetic energy, and that kinetic energy is not changing during the plateau, the temperature stays at 0 °C throughout freezing. Only once every particle has become part of the solid structure does further cooling start to lower the kinetic energy — and the temperature — again.
